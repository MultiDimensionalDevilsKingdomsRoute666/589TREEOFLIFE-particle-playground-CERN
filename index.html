05 — Knowledge

What You Can Learn Here

Particle Playground is designed to teach the same concepts, methods, and data analysis techniques used by physicists at CERN — structured for curious minds without prior physics training.

01
The Standard Model of Particle Physics

The Standard Model is humanity's most precise description of the fundamental building blocks of matter and the forces that govern them. Through the Particle Zoo, you encounter all 17 known fundamental particles — 6 quarks, 6 leptons, 4 gauge bosons, and the Higgs boson — each with their experimentally measured properties sourced from the Particle Data Group 2024 Review. You learn why quarks are permanently confined inside hadrons, how leptons differ from quarks, and what it means for a particle to be its own antiparticle.

02
How Particle Accelerators Work

The Large Hadron Collider accelerates protons to 99.9999991% the speed of light inside a 27 km superconducting ring buried 100 metres underground. The Collider section teaches you what "centre-of-mass energy" really means, why luminosity determines how rare a process you can observe, and why different beam types — proton-proton vs. lead-lead — answer completely different physics questions. You control the same parameters that LHC operators tune in real run configurations.

03
Quantum Field Theory & Cross-Sections

Every time you press COLLIDE, the physics engine samples from production cross-sections calculated using Next-to-Leading Order (NLO) Quantum Chromodynamics. The Physics Prediction panel shows you why QCD jet production dominates at low energies while the Higgs boson requires teraelectronvolt-scale collisions — a direct consequence of E = mc² and the mass hierarchy of fundamental particles. You develop an intuition for why "cross-section" is the language physicists use to quantify probability in the quantum world.

04
Feynman Diagrams & Interaction Notation

Feynman diagrams are the universal shorthand of particle physics — each one is not merely an illustration but a precise mathematical term in a perturbation expansion. The simulator generates the correct diagram for each collision type: quark-gluon exchange for QCD jets, the gluon-fusion top-quark loop for Higgs production, and the quark-antiquark annihilation vertex for Z boson events. You learn to read time flowing left to right, to distinguish fermion lines from boson propagators, and to understand why each diagram topology corresponds to a different physical process.

05
Particle Detection & Event Reconstruction

The Real Events section shows authentic CMS detector data from proton-proton collisions at 7 TeV in 2010. You learn to read a transverse event display: how charged tracks curve in a 3.8 Tesla solenoidal magnetic field (and what the radius of curvature tells you about momentum), how hadronic jets appear as energy cones in the calorimeters, why photons leave no tracking signal, and how physicists infer the presence of invisible neutrinos from missing transverse momentum. These are the same reconstruction techniques used in publications from the CMS and ATLAS collaborations.

06
The Invariant Mass Method — How Particles Are Discovered

The Mass Spectrum section teaches you the single most important technique in experimental particle physics: the invariant mass resonance. By accumulating many collision events and plotting the calculated mass of particle pairs, a peak appears above the smooth background at the mass of the parent particle. This is how the Z boson was discovered at CERN in 1983, how the Higgs boson was found in 2012, and how physicists continue to search for new particles today. The formula M² = (E₁+E₂)² − |p⃗₁+p⃗₂|² gives a Lorentz-invariant quantity that is the same in any reference frame — a profound consequence of special relativity.

07
Open Science & Reproducibility

Every number in this application can be traced to a peer-reviewed source. Particle properties come from the PDG 2024 Review, collision events from the CERN Open Data Portal (CC0 license), and cross-sections from published NLO calculations. This reflects a core principle of modern science: results must be reproducible from publicly available data. CERN has released over 2 petabytes of collision data freely to the world, enabling anyone — student, educator, or independent researcher — to perform the same analyses that won Nobel Prizes.
